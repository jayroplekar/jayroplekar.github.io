Supporting neurodivergent adults — ADHD & autism spectrum A communication guide

What not to do
— and what to do instead.

For a high-intelligence, neurodivergent adult, well-intentioned support often backfires. Understanding the "why" — and the better alternative — is what makes the difference.

Common mistakes — why they backfire — what to do instead
Common mistake
Why it backfires
What to do instead
Mistake 01
Sharing articles or productivity tips
Why it backfires
Signals you think they haven't already considered this. For someone with a 140 IQ, it reads as an insult dressed as help.
Do this instead
Ask what's worked for them before, even partially. Let them lead — "What have you tried that felt okay?"
Mistake 02
Offering to help them make a schedule
Why it backfires
Immediately positions you as the authority. They disengage or comply performatively — neither leads to real change.
Do this instead
Ask what a good day would feel like to them. Let them design their own structure — even imperfect, it's theirs.
Mistake 03
Praising compliance too much
Why it backfires
Creates performance pressure and feels patronising. They start doing things for your approval — or stop doing them to assert autonomy.
Do this instead
Acknowledge neutrally and move on. "Cool." No score-keeping, no gold stars. Consistency matters more than recognition.
Mistake 04
Following up on whether they did the thing
Why it backfires
Triggers shame and avoidance. If they didn't do it, your follow-up confirms their worst fears about themselves — and about you.
Do this instead
If they bring it up, respond with curiosity — "What got in the way?" If they don't, let it go. Trust the process.
Mistake 05
Framing structure as "what successful people do"
Why it backfires
Activates deep rejection of neurotypical norms. They've spent a lifetime being told to fit a mould that doesn't fit. This shuts the conversation down instantly.
Do this instead
Anchor structure to their values, not external success. "What would a day feel like if it worked for you?" — not for anyone else.
The goal is safety,
not compliance.
When they feel genuinely understood — not managed — they'll start bringing questions to you. That's when real change becomes possible.

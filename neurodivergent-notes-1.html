You're Holding the Pencil Wrong

On the moment you realize the correction was never about the pencil.

Jay Roplekar

I was asked to sit with a therapist once, and somewhere in that first hour she said: "Have you ever considered that you might not have a working memory problem, but a working memory that works differently?" I had spent three decades being corrected. Corrected by teachers who wanted me to organize my papers. Corrected by colleagues who wanted me to organize my thoughts linearly. Corrected by myself, with increasing efficiency, because the correction had become automatic. The thing you get asked to fix long enough starts to feel less like a request and more like evidence.

But a working memory that works differently — that phrasing split something. It suggested that the architecture of my cognition was not broken, just orthogonal. Same task, same goal, different wiring. The analogy I kept coming back to was left-handedness.

I am not left-handed, but left-handed children used to be forced to write with their right hands. For decades. This was not optional. Teachers would tie their left hands behind their backs, or rap them with a ruler. The reasoning was explicit: left-handedness was sinister — in the literal sense, the Latin root — and needed to be corrected. The children were not deficient. They were inconvenient.

· · ·
"The corrector and the corrected have a shared assumption: that there is a right way, and deviations from it are problems to be fixed."

What happened to those children is instructive. Many became functionally ambidextrous, able to write right-handed when forced to, but slower, less fluent, more effortful. They reported lasting effects — fatigue, reduced handwriting legibility, hand pain. They had adapted to the environment instead of the environment adapting to them. The gap between adaptation and accommodation is everything.

Adaptation means: I am changing myself to fit the world. Accommodation means: the world is changing slightly (in almost inconsequential way) to allow me to exist with a different approach. One is chosen, contextual, and reversible. The other is compelled, chronic, and it wears you down.

The engineering view

In manufacturing, parts have tolerances. A dimension specified at 10mm ± 0.1mm will fit the assembly correctly whether it measures 9.9mm or 10.1mm. A part that measures 9.85mm is out of spec — but that's not a defect in the part. It's a failure in the design process. The spec was written for a different component, or the design is brittle.

Being told to hold the pencil differently is being out of spec. Whether that's a defect or a design problem depends entirely on who gets to write the spec.

The distinction I want to be precise about: I do not think my wiring is superior. I think the correctors and I share an assumption that there is one right way to hold the pencil, and if you are not doing it, you need fixing. That assumption is the thing. The actual holding of the pencil — that is neutral.

What changed in the therapy session was not my working memory. It was the question. Instead of "how do I fix this," the question became "what does this do well?" And that is a different problem entirely. Not fix the part. Understand the part. Then design a system where the part's particular shape fits.

I am still being corrected, sometimes. It is harder now to hear correction without also hearing: I was told I was doing things wrong. I was not doing things wrong. I was doing them differently, and the world was built for someone else, and I spent many years internalizing that as a personal failure. Left-handed children taught me that. They did not have a writing problem. They had an environment problem.
